In the context of **Agenda-Setting Theory** (originally developed by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw in 1968), an **"agenda"** is simply **a prioritized list of issues, topics, or events that are deemed important at any given time.** 

The core premise of the theory is best summarized by political scientist Bernard Cohen’s famous quote: *“The press may not be successful much of the time in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers **what to think about**.”* 

In this theory, the "agenda" is exactly that: the list of things we are thinking about. 

To fully understand the theory, you must understand that there isn't just one agenda. There are **three distinct but interacting agendas**:

### 1. The Media Agenda
This is the set of topics and issues that news organizations, journalists, and editors decide to cover prominently. 
* **How it is measured:** By looking at the length of a story, its placement (e.g., front page vs. page 10, or the lead story on a TV broadcast), and the frequency of coverage over time.
* **Example:** If major news outlets run headline stories about inflation every day for a month, inflation is at the top of the *Media Agenda*.

### 2. The Public Agenda
This is the set of issues that the general public believes are the most important.
* **How it is measured:** Usually through public opinion polls and surveys asking questions like, "What do you think is the most important problem facing the country today?"
* **Example:** If 70% of the public answers "the economy and inflation" to that poll question, then inflation is at the top of the *Public Agenda*.

### 3. The Policy (or Political) Agenda
This is the list of issues that government officials, lawmakers, and policymakers are actively paying attention to and trying to legislate or act upon.
* **How it is measured:** By looking at bills introduced in Congress, topics of presidential speeches, or issues debated in legislative sessions.
* **Example:** If the government passes an emergency bill to lower interest rates and curb prices, inflation is at the top of the *Policy Agenda*.

---

### How the Agendas Interact (The Agenda-Setting Process)
The theory studies how items move from one agenda to another. The classic flow is:
**Media Agenda $\rightarrow$ Public Agenda $\rightarrow$ Policy Agenda**

1. The media decides to heavily cover an issue (Media Agenda).
2. Because the public sees it constantly, they begin to perceive it as a massive problem (Public Agenda).
3. Because the public is demanding action, politicians are forced to address it and pass laws (Policy Agenda).

### Beyond the Basic "Agenda" (First vs. Second Level)
As the theory evolved, researchers realized that an "agenda" consists of more than just a list of broad topics.

* **First-Level Agenda Setting (The "What"):** This is the traditional list of issues (e.g., healthcare, immigration, climate change). It tells us *what* to think about.
* **Second-Level Agenda Setting (The "How"):** Also known as **Framing**, this refers to the *attributes* or specific details of an issue that the media highlights. For example, if the topic is "climate change," the media might frame it as an *economic* issue (cost of green energy) or a *survival* issue (extreme weather). This tells us *how* to think about the issue. 

### Summary
In Agenda-Setting Theory, an **agenda** is essentially a measure of **salience** (importance). If an issue is on the agenda, it means it has captured the attention of the media, the public, or the government. The theory explores who has the power to write that list.
