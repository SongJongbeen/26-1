To answer your question directly: **You can hypothesize a link to trust, but you cannot claim it as the primary cause without addressing significant confounding variables.**

In academic research, the decrease in news citations is more likely to be attributed to the **evolution of scholarly standards** and **information infrastructure** rather than a reflection of researchers' personal trust in news organizations.

Here is a breakdown of how you should frame this analysis to be academically rigorous:

### 1. The "Academic Atmosphere" Argument (More Likely)
Academic norms have shifted significantly over the last few decades. If news citations are decreasing, it is likely due to:

*   **The "Primary Source" Mandate:** Peer-review standards have become stricter. In the past, a researcher might cite a *New York Times* article about a scientific discovery. Today, reviewers expect the citation of the original peer-reviewed paper that the news article was reporting on.
*   **The Proliferation of Journals:** There are vastly more academic journals today than 30 years ago. In the past, news outlets were often the first to report on social trends. Now, there is likely a "pre-print" or an open-access journal article covering almost any niche topic, making news citations unnecessary.
*   **Digital Persistence (DOIs):** Academics are trained to cite sources with permanent identifiers (DOIs). News URLs are notorious for "link rot" (changing or disappearing). Scholars avoid news sources to ensure their reference lists remain functional for decades.
*   **Formalization of "Grey Literature":** Reports from NGOs, think tanks (Pew, Brookings), and government agencies have become more accessible online. These are seen as "halfway" between news and journals—more authoritative than a news clip but faster than a book.

### 2. The "Trust in News" Argument (The Sociological Angle)
You *can* relate your findings to trust, but you must define "trust" in a specific way. It is likely not that academics think journalists are lying, but rather:

*   **Epistemic Reliability:** Academics may perceive news as increasingly "shallow" or "sensationalized" compared to the depth required for a citation.
*   **Polarization:** If a researcher cites *Fox News* or *MSNBC*, they may fear they are signaling a political bias to their peer reviewers. To remain "objective," they avoid news citations entirely to prevent their work from being dismissed as partisan.
*   **The "Fake News" Era:** Even if a news source is reputable, the general cultural anxiety regarding "misinformation" may lead academics to "over-correct" by stripping their bibliographies of anything that isn't a peer-reviewed study.

### 3. How to Structure Your Research
If you want to publish this, you should test for **"Substitution Effects."** 

If news citations are going down, what is going up?
*   **Scenario A:** News citations are down, but citations of **Pre-prints/Working Papers** are up. *Conclusion:* This is about speed. Academics used to use news for "current events," but now they use fast-tracked academic papers.
*   **Scenario B:** News citations are down, and citations of **Think Tank/NGO reports** are up. *Conclusion:* This is about authority. Academics are seeking data-heavy sources over narrative-heavy news.
*   **Scenario C:** News citations are down across all fields, including political science and sociology. *Conclusion:* This supports your "Trust/Polarization" theory, as these fields traditionally rely on news for case studies.

### Summary
If you simply say "citations are down, therefore trust is down," your peer reviewers will likely reject the claim as a **non-sequitur**. 

Instead, frame it as: *"The decreasing reliance on journalistic sources in scholarly bibliographies: A shift in academic standards or a reflection of the broader 'crisis of trust' in media?"* 

This allows you to explore the "Academic Atmosphere" as the primary driver while using "Trust" as a compelling sociological lens to explain the data.
